=== PART A: Treasure Hunt (exact factored value iteration) ===

Model. The state is (treasure mask, ship cell, pirate-1 cell, pirate-2 cell); the two
pirate regions are the 4-connected components of the pirate area. From the layout and
probability files we build the exact model env.py uses: the ship takes the intended
direction w.p. ps and each other direction w.p. (1-ps)/3 (staying put if blocked); each
pirate moves independently by its own stay-or-move matrix inside its region; outcomes
are checked in env.py's order (pirate hit, then fort, then treasure).

Algorithm. Synchronous value iteration, with every Bellman backup factored into three
cheap stages instead of one dense transition matrix:
 1. Landing values U = R + gamma*V(next) for every landing cell and pirate pair
    (collecting a treasure clears its bit; fort and pirate hits are terminal).
 2. Pirate expectation: two single-threaded einsums with P1 and P2, since the pirates
    move independently. Cost O(S*(R1+R2)) instead of O(S*R1*R2).
 3. Ship expectation: since Q[a] = q*sum_d G_d + (ps-q)*G_a with q=(1-ps)/3, max and
    argmax over actions need only a running sum and a running best G_a.

Anytime behaviour and time safety.
 - First we solve the pirate-free relaxation over (mask, cell) in milliseconds. Its
   values and greedy actions, broadcast over all pirate positions, are the starting V
   and policy. A sensible policy exists even if no exact sweep finishes, and VI still
   converges to the same V* from this start.
 - Sweeps stop at convergence (max|dV| < 1e-9), at 0.85*T, or when the next sweep is
   predicted (1.5 x the last sweep time) to overrun.
 - learn_policy catches run.py's TimeoutException and only ever installs complete
   arrays, so the last complete policy survives. get_action is an O(1) lookup.

Validation. Against brute-force VI on the unfactored MDP built from env.py's own
functions: max |dV| <= 4e-9 on tests 1-3 and <= 1e-7 on 40 random layouts, with no
suboptimal actions; Monte-Carlo returns from env.py match V(start). Scores (1000
runs): TC1 6.07, TC2 1.73, TC3 7.35, i.e. the optimal V*(start) = 6.068 / 1.747 / 7.149
(TA baselines as updated on Piazza after the run.py discounting fix: ~6.0 / ~1.7; the
PDF's ~8 / ~4.8 predate it). Realistic grids converge in under 1 s; 48 random grids (N
up to 30, up to 31.5M states) ran under SIGALRM with no timeouts or crashes.
Gauss-Seidel and a sparse pirate stencil were measured slower with numpy.

=== PART B: Highway (tabular Q-learning) ===

Representation. Q(s, a) over the observation (speed, lane, min_dist[0..3]): 4*4*5^4 =
10,000 states x 5 actions, Q0 = 0, stored as plain Python lists (cheaper than numpy for
5-element rows). Every training episode uses a fresh HighwayEnv(), as evaluation does
(reset() spawns traffic around the old car position). Only env.step() rewards are used;
no reward values are assumed anywhere, and gamma comes from the constructor.

Collision credit. env.step() checks for a collision BEFORE applying the chosen action,
so the penalty returned with done belongs to the previous action. Each update is
delayed by one step: at done, the previous (s, a) gets target r_t + gamma*r_{t+1} (no
bootstrap) and the done step's own (s, a), whose action never ran, is not updated. At
the 1000-step limit run.py's scoring also stops, so the same target is exact there.

Schedules (all chosen by measurement: >= 3 runs x 300 episodes per setting).
 - Epsilon-greedy, eps linear from 1 to 0.01 over the whole budget T. Training stops
   at 0.85*T, so the last eps is about 0.16. Exploring to the end clearly beat faster
   decays (reaching 0.01 at 0.2*T: 2.70 vs 3.87 at T=60).
 - Step size alpha linear from 0.5 to 0.25 over the first 3M environment steps, then
   constant. The best constant alpha depends on the budget (0.5 at T=60, 0.3 at
   T=240); counting experience in steps rather than seconds gives a large alpha early
   and a smaller one with more data. It beat every constant and time-based schedule.

Why a large alpha matters (main finding). Every non-collision reward is positive, so
true values are about 3-12 at gamma=0.99 while Q starts at 0. With bootstrapping that
pessimistic bias shrinks only by a factor (1 - alpha*(1-gamma)) per update (about 1000
updates at alpha=0.1), while the non-bootstrapped crash target is learned in about 10
updates. Rarely visited fast states therefore look worse than they are and the agent
stays slow. A diagnostic run with Q0 = +3 (not used, since it assumes reward
knowledge) raised the T=60 score from 3.93 to 6.52 at alpha=0.1, confirming this. A
large alpha propagates the positive values much faster.

Learned behaviour. At gamma=0.99 the policy drives fast and accepts late crashes
(median crash step ~350-410, costing only ~0.4 of discounted return). At gamma=0.999 it
drives cautiously (~7% crashes); at gamma <= 0.95 it races, as the score settles early.

Time safety. A time.monotonic() deadline, plus a catch of run.py's TimeoutException; Q
stays valid under interruption (300 random interruptions tested).

Results (gamma=0.99, mean of 3 runs x 300 episodes):
  T (s)  :   5     10    20    30    60    240
  score  :  2.8   3.2   4.0   4.2   5.3   7.0-7.1
TA baseline at 240 s: ~6 (Piazza update; PDF: ~5.0). Scores rise with T at every gamma
tested (0.5 to 0.999).

Tried and rejected (measured): constant eps; eps/alpha decaying over a fixed number of
steps; training to 0.95*T; an optimistic start from the agent's own probe episodes.
